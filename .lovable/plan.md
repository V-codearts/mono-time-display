# Your Design Handwriting — A Reusable Creative Brief

This brief synthesizes your requests, corrections, and established rules. The named philosophy is an interpretation of those choices, not a claim that you previously used these exact words. Later decisions take precedence over earlier experiments. The principles apply beyond websites; the final reference section preserves the specific measurements of your current work.

## 1. The central philosophy: quiet, exact, object-first

My design language is **monochrome, typographic, spatially precise, and deliberately restrained**. It is not minimalism achieved by making something generic or unfinished. It is minimalism achieved by deciding exactly what deserves to exist, removing what does not, and refining the relationships between what remains.

The subject is the main event. The surrounding design should establish a recognizable frame of mind without competing with the object, image, text, or experience being presented. Identity comes from repeated decisions about type, space, proportion, motion, and restraint—not from accumulating graphic decoration.

The result should feel controlled and intentional rather than friendly, busy, ornamental, or eager to explain itself. It should be quiet without being weak, technical without becoming a cluttered control panel, and distinctive without needing a conspicuous visual gimmick.

**Reduction must increase precision. Fewer elements mean each remaining element matters more.**

## 2. Color: a neutral field, not a decorative palette

Use black, near-white, and gray as the core visual vocabulary. My established dark background is **#131313**; the corresponding light background is **#FEFEFE**. These exact near-black and near-white values matter in the current identity, particularly where moving imagery must merge seamlessly into its surroundings.

Gray is functional: it distinguishes secondary, inactive, or less immediate information. Strong foreground contrast identifies the active subject or state. Do not use additional colors merely to make the design more exciting.

The presentation system is monochrome; that does not automatically mean every photograph or featured object must be converted to grayscale. The surrounding frame should remain neutral so the subject can retain its own identity.

Light and dark versions must feel like the same composition under opposite conditions, not two unrelated art directions. Preserve positions, proportions, typography, and behavior. Where imagery or animation is theme-specific, it must match the surrounding field without seams or flashes.

Avoid decorative gradients, colored glows, aura effects, and visual atmospheres that substitute for composition. You explicitly removed the aura; restraint is the identity, not an effect layered over it.

## 3. Typography: consistent, uppercase, monospace

Use **Roboto Mono** as the defining typeface. Visible wording is **strictly ALL CAPS**. The consistent monospace rhythm is part of the handwriting: letters, numbers, symbols, and labels should feel like members of one system.

Do not introduce an expressive display face, a serif, or a second typographic personality to manufacture hierarchy. Establish hierarchy through placement, space, contrast, state, and carefully controlled weight before resorting to large changes in size.

The existing reference size is **16px** for ordinary text. Information text should match the scale of the central PORTFOLIO text rather than becoming tiny incidental metadata. Symbols can be larger when they need a clear presence, but they must still belong to the same visual family.

Normal weight is the baseline. Boldness is a restrained interaction cue, not an excuse to make everything emphatic. Interactive text and glyphs can become bold when engaged; they do not need a colored pill, filled panel, or ornamental outline to announce themselves.

Use short, literal wording. Titles and descriptions occupy separate lines when needed. Do not add marketing language or explanatory prose simply because empty space exists. Your established image-description rule also excludes mentions of specific materials, such as cotton; that is a content constraint, not a universal ban on discussing materials in every unrelated project.

## 4. Composition: open space with exact relationships

Treat negative space as an active part of the composition. It isolates the subject, creates calm, and makes a small number of controls legible. Do not fill empty areas with decoration to make the work appear more complete.

The subject should usually have a clear central presence. Peripheral controls occupy predictable, balanced positions. In the website, the upper-left navigation glyph and upper-right theme dot act as paired anchors around an otherwise open field.

Spacing should follow relationships, not arbitrary offsets. One of your clearest rules is that, on smaller screens, the distance from a control's center to the outer page edge should equal the distance from that center to the adjacent image edge. The control sits at the midpoint of the available margin. This is more specific than simply saying “make it symmetrical.”

Preserve this mindset in other media: align edges deliberately; balance the space on either side of an anchor; use consistent centers and baselines; and judge the gaps between elements as carefully as the elements themselves.

Do not add borders around objects or images unless a real functional requirement demands them. You explicitly removed the image border. Avoid floating panels and decorative frames that turn the subject into a boxed interface accessory.

An object's scale must be judged by its visual weight, not only by the available canvas size. Your smaller archive object was deliberately reduced on larger displays. Bigger is not automatically better; the right surrounding space can give a small object more authority.

## 5. Controls: the smallest meaningful sign

Prefer a minimal vocabulary of familiar symbols: **+**, **−**, a back arrow, and a small solid dot. They should read as punctuation within the composition, not as oversized interface widgets.

A control should communicate its purpose through its symbol, position, state, and response. Avoid adding a rectangular button, background, or long label when the existing sign is sufficient.

State changes should be economical and continuous. The plus becomes a minus when an area is expanded; the navigation glyph becomes a back arrow while inspecting an object. These are transformations of an existing anchor, not the appearance of a second unrelated control somewhere else.

Maintain exact coordinates when the role of a control changes. A back action should not make the user search for a new location. Control dimensions and alignments must stay stable as their weight or symbol changes.

Minimal appearance does not mean ambiguous behavior. Actual objects and actual controls respond; empty surroundings should not act as oversized invisible targets. A non-interactive label should remain visually secondary rather than pretending to be a working action.

## 6. Information: reveal it without overwhelming the object

Start with the subject, not a full inventory of explanations. Secondary information is revealed intentionally rather than being permanently displayed around the object.

When information opens, the composition must make genuine room for it. Do not paste text over imagery or allow controls, text, and objects to collide. The object and its information should feel like a single coordinated arrangement.

Your info trigger initially occupies the midpoint between the image's lower edge and the bottom of the display. Once expanded, it becomes a minus and moves into a precise relationship with the image. Information then appears as separate, regularly spaced uppercase rows beneath it.

Responsive adaptation should preserve priorities rather than simply shrinking everything equally. In your current reference, a small-screen image can first move upward within a defined limit and then shrink if more room is needed. On desktop, the image's top position remains anchored while its scale changes. These different responses protect the same underlying composition.

When the information is closed, restore the original arrangement smoothly. When the viewer itself is left, do not unnecessarily replay the entire information-closing choreography; a straightforward fade can be the more restrained choice.

## 7. Motion: spatial logic, not entertainment

Animation is part of the design language, but it must describe a change rather than decorate a static layout. Every movement should have an understandable origin, destination, direction, and order.

Navigation entries slide in from beyond the edge in sequence. Closing reverses their order. Information rows follow a related staggered rhythm. These movements make hierarchy perceptible without introducing extra graphic elements.

Do not replace this with indiscriminate fading. For menu entries and information rows, your later preference is **slide-only entry and exit**. Fades have specific uses: a restrained departure, the plus/minus crossfade, or material already authored inside a video. They are not a universal transition applied to everything.

Page changes form one continuous vertical event: the current composition moves upward while the next arrives from below. Objects extending beyond the visible boundary must clear it coherently rather than being abruptly clipped.

Matched controls must travel equivalent distances with matching timing. You corrected the asymmetric entrance of the navigation glyph and theme dot because near-symmetry was not sufficient.

Use deliberate easing and brief, controlled timing. Motion should feel smooth but decisive—not springy, elastic, theatrical, or needlessly prolonged. Different actions can have different rhythms; consistency means a shared logic, not forcing every animation to use the same duration.

## 8. Object transitions: bespoke and uninterrupted

Where an object needs a distinctive transformation, use an authored transition rather than imposing the same generic slide on every state. Your teeth-replica variation switch uses videos that align with the object, with appropriate forward, backward, light, and dark versions.

Transitions should respect the exact current object layout, including its information-expanded position and scale. No intermediate state should jump back to an old arrangement.

Do not expose the mechanics of the swap. The next still image should not appear prematurely, the previous image should not blink away before the transition starts, and the first interaction should not hesitate while later interactions feel instant.

Return-to-initial-state transitions deserve the same care as forward transitions. A reset is part of the object's language, not an afterthought. If a theme change would break continuity during playback, hold that state stable until the transformation completes.

## 9. Finish: the seams must disappear

Performance is perceptual design. First-use delay, flickering, twitching symbols, late image resizing, mismatched backgrounds, and tiny rendering seams all weaken the intended sense of control.

Prepare imagery and motion before they are needed. Keep dimensions stable. Preserve the user's position when returning from inspecting an object. Remember persistent choices such as the light/dark setting.

The introductory video already contains its own fading; do not layer another fade over it. The surrounding presentation should hand off cleanly. Edge masking in the current intro exists to hide rendering artifacts, not to create a visible decorative border.

Hidden scrollbars and disabled text selection are established website choices that reduce visible browser interference. For another medium, carry over the goal—an uncluttered presentation—not these mechanics automatically. A writing or editing tool may require text selection to function properly.

An earlier requested idle behavior dims interface text after 13 seconds and restores it immediately on interaction. Treat that as a context-dependent ambient behavior, not a mandatory effect for static work or every future application.

## 10. Translating the handwriting beyond websites

- **Posters and print:** A restrained monochrome field, one clear subject, uppercase Roboto Mono, measured margins, and little supporting text. No need to imitate a navigation menu.
- **Motion graphics:** Precise directional transitions, restrained staggering, matched start/end positions, seamless backgrounds, and symbols that transform rather than multiply.
- **Product presentations:** Show the object clearly, give it proportionate breathing room, and reveal detail in a measured hierarchy instead of surrounding it with badges and promotional claims.
- **Applications and interactive installations:** Keep essential controls few and predictable; reveal secondary information deliberately; preserve spatial continuity and immediate response.
- **Physical graphics or packaging:** Translate the same type, contrast, spacing, and hierarchy into the medium. Do not copy screen-specific pixel measurements as if they were physical dimensions.

The goal is **the same decision-making handwriting**, not a literal reproduction of the website. Recognizability should survive even when the medium has no menu, no theme toggle, and no animation.

## 11. Reference specifications—not universal constants

These values describe your existing work and can guide related interactive projects:

- Dark field: #131313. Light field: #FEFEFE.
- Roboto Mono, uppercase throughout; ordinary text 16px.
- Information rows: 26px spacing; final row 26px from the bottom.
- Expanded minus: 21px below the image bottom.
- Small-screen upward image limit: 81px from the top before further shrinking.
- Navigation glyph/theme entrance and page slides: 600ms; clock entrance: 500ms.
- Plus/minus crossfade: approximately 180ms in the current controls.
- Information rows: 400ms slide, 80ms stagger, reversed on closure.
- Expanded-information departure: 220ms fade rather than a full reverse sequence.
- Current intro handoff: 1720ms, with fading authored into the video; this timing belongs to that asset, not every future intro.
- Clock: unpadded numbers separated by spaces; local time; date order appropriate to the user's locale. No colons between hours, minutes, and seconds.

Older experiments involving extra intro fades, colon-separated time, MORE navigation, and an aura are not instructions to reproduce the final style. Deployment settings and menu names are project details, not design philosophy.

## 12. Copy-ready instruction for another AI

“Apply my visual handwriting rather than a generic minimalist template. Use a monochrome presentation built around #131313, #FEFEFE, and functional grays. Use Roboto Mono and strictly uppercase wording. Keep the subject primary and the surrounding design quiet. Build identity through exact spacing, balanced margins, stable anchors, restrained weight changes, and consistent proportions—not decorative effects.

“Use only elements that serve a purpose. Avoid auras, ornamental borders, decorative gradients, unnecessary boxes, oversized controls, and filler copy. Favor a few meaningful symbols where interaction exists. Reveal secondary information intentionally and make real space for it without overlapping the subject.

“Where motion exists, give it spatial logic: clear directions, matched travel, controlled easing, orderly staggering, and coherent reverse behavior. Use fades selectively. Bespoke object transformations must align exactly with their still states and begin without first-use delay, flicker, or layout jumps.

“Adapt these principles to the medium. Do not copy website controls or pixel measurements into a non-website project without a reason. The finished result should feel quiet, precise, object-first, and deliberately controlled. If an element cannot justify its presence through function, hierarchy, or composition, remove it.”
